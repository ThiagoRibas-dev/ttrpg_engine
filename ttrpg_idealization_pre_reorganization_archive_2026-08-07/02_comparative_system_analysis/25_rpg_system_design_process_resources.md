# RPG System Design Process Resources

**Status:** Research resource catalogue.  
**Purpose:** Record external guides, essays, and lessons relevant to designing, calibrating, documenting, and playtesting a tabletop RPG system. These resources do not override canonical rules.

## Vincent Baker — RPG Design and Development

Vincent Baker’s design essays on creating Powered by the Apocalypse games are useful as process and theory references, not as mechanical templates for this project.

Relevant themes include:

- Starting from intended play experience.
- Identifying the underlying model of action and consequence.
- Designing procedures that express the game’s priorities.
- Drafting a new RPG from an existing game without copying its assumptions.
- Distinguishing specific mechanics from the design model beneath them.

Useful reference: [Powered by the Apocalypse, Part 1](https://lumpley.games/2019/12/30/powered-by-the-apocalypse-part-1/)

## D&D Next Playtest Lessons

The D&D Next playtest is a useful case study in large-scale iterative design. Relevant lessons include:

- Test mathematical balance and thematic coherence separately.
- Examine player expectations as well as intended rules behavior.
- Stress-test a system after stripping away its presentation and content trappings.
- Use repeated play and feedback to identify contradictions and friction.
- Treat playtest evidence as evidence for design decisions, not as automatic authority.

Useful discussion: [The Role of Playtesting in Game Design](https://www.gamesprecipice.com/role-of-playtesting/)

## Process Lessons Adopted for This Project

The resources reinforce the value of separating:

```text
Design Intent
Mathematical Evidence
Mechanical Rule
Content Implementation
Playtest Evidence
Final Decision
```

They also support the current project workflow:

1. Establish design intent.
2. Define the mathematical model.
3. Build a minimal prototype.
4. Stress-test the prototype.
5. Observe actual player and GM behavior.
6. Separate mechanical failure from presentation failure.
7. Revise and consolidate only after review.

## Application to the Current Project

The most relevant process principles are:

- Keep the foundational framework separate from Classes, Feats, Spells, Ancestries, and other content.
- Calculate probabilities before finalizing target bands.
- Use representative profiles rather than isolated examples.
- Treat Equipment as an independent progression vector.
- Test multiple Difficulty Vectors with similar probabilities but different capability gates.
- Preserve rejected designs as historical research.
- Do not let research artifacts become accidental rules.
- Avoid solving content problems by adding framework exceptions.
- Use playtesting only after the base framework and MVP content library are sufficiently stable.

## Public Designer and Community Process Resources

### Matt Colville / MCDM

MCDM's public development material is useful for studying iterative tactical-system design, monster design, encounter usability, and the difference between a mechanically functional option and an option that creates meaningful play. MCDM describes an ongoing series documenting development of its own RPG, `Designing the Game`.

References:

- [MCDM press and development information](https://www.mcdmproductions.com/press)
- [MCDM about page](https://www.mcdmproductions.com/about)

Transferable lessons for this project:

- Build monsters around interesting actions, not only numerical statistics.
- Make combatants change the battlefield.
- Give Classes distinctive tactical identities.
- Test whether a mechanic creates decisions rather than only increasing output.
- Design bosses as a different play experience from ordinary enemies.
- Check whether the GM can actually operate the content at the table.

### Kelsey Dionne / Shadowdark

Kelsey Dionne's Shadowdark design discussions are useful for studying design pillars, iterative removal of mechanics, and alignment between rules and intended play. In a retrospective discussion, Dionne describes starting from table experience, identifying what she liked and disliked, distilling design pillars, testing a skill system, and removing it when it conflicted with the game's intended ethos.

References:

- [Deconstructing and Rebuilding RPG Design](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OADZLovtpPg)
- [The Arcane Library / Shadowdark Blog](https://www.thearcanelibrary.com/blogs/shadowdark-blog)

Transferable lessons:

- A mechanic can be clever and compatible while still being wrong for the intended experience.
- Design pillars should be checked against every major subsystem.
- Removing a mechanic is a valid design success, not a failure.
- Rules should not be added merely to appear adjacent to another game.
- Complexity should justify itself through play value.

### Ben Milton / Questing Beast

Ben Milton's OSR and post-OSR design discussions are useful for studying player judgment, tactical infinity, procedures, and the boundary between rules and GM adjudication.

References:

- [Rule One](https://questingbeast.substack.com/p/rule-one)
- [The Glatisant newsletter](https://questingbeast.substack.com/p/the-glatisant-issue-9)

Transferable lessons:

- Players should be able to affect the fictional world by describing what their characters do.
- Rules should support meaningful in-world choices rather than classify every possible action.
- A broad Activity procedure may be better than another narrow Skill.
- A rule should exist because it improves play, not merely because the designer wants a category.
- GM-facing procedures are part of a complete RPG system.

These lessons do not require adopting OSR minimalism. They are process and usability considerations.

## Forum Anecdotes as Evidence

Forum discussions are useful qualitative evidence but are not authoritative design theory. They can reveal recurring failure modes such as:

- Character creation becoming too long.
- Simple-looking rules creating table confusion.
- Modifiers being forgotten.
- Players optimizing in unexpected ways.
- Intended subsystems being ignored.
- Mathematically balanced mechanics feeling bad in play.
- Rules requiring too much GM interpretation.
- Playtesters misunderstanding the designer's assumptions.

# Actionable Design Guidance for This Project

This section converts the case studies and community lessons into practices for our own technical and documentation workflow.

## 1. Technical Design Workflow

### 1.1 Define the job of a mechanic first

Before designing a subsystem, write a short statement answering:

```text
What player decision does this mechanic create?
What fictional relationship does it model?
What resource, risk, or trade-off does it introduce?
What existing mechanic does it replace or complement?
What would be lost if we removed it?
```

This reflects the design-pillar approach discussed by Kelsey Dionne, whose Shadowdark retrospective describes using broad design pillars to identify when a skill system or unnecessary inventory rules were working against the intended experience.[1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OADZLovtpPg)

### 1.2 Separate the mathematical vectors

For every subsystem, identify which mathematical vector it changes:

```text
Die Size:
  Numerical ceiling or scale.

Dice Pool Size:
  Reliability and number of available results.

Difficulty Vector:
  Thresholds the results must satisfy.

Automatic Success:
  Guaranteed threshold satisfaction.

Action Cost:
  Opportunity cost during the turn.

Resource Cost:
  In-world endurance or availability pressure.

Permission:
  What the actor is allowed to attempt.

Requirement:
  What must be true before attempting it.
```

Do not let one subsystem silently modify several vectors unless the design intent explicitly calls for that. When it does, document the interaction as a separate rule.

### 1.3 Build a minimal mathematical model

Before writing a large content catalogue:

1. Define the smallest working version of the subsystem.
2. Calculate its raw probabilities or resource costs.
3. Test ordinary, optimized, and adversarial inputs.
4. Identify edge cases.
5. Only then add Traits, Feats, Equipment, and special permissions.

For the current resolution engine, this means testing:

```text
Die Size × Dice Pool × Difficulty Vector
```

before adding Classes, Feats, Equipment, or spells that modify the result.

### 1.4 Test vectors, not only averages

A subsystem should be tested across:

- Weak and strong Attributes.
- Untrained and fully invested Skills.
- Baseline and equipped pools.
- Equal and unequal opposed pools.
- Ordinary and extreme Difficulty Vectors.
- Boons and Banes.
- Automatic Successes.
- Low-tier and high-tier actors.

Do not rely on one average character or one average roll. The current probability artifacts follow this principle by separating raw dice, pool volume, die size, vectors, equipment profiles, and opposed contests.

### 1.5 Treat outliers as diagnostic, not automatically as targets

The D&D 3.5e monster aggregation is useful for identifying ranges and outliers, but an extreme monster should not automatically define the expected curve for all monsters at that Challenge Rating.

For any dataset, record:

```text
Mean
Median, where available
Range
Sample size
Outliers
Role distribution
```

This avoids designing the baseline around an exceptional dragon, spellcaster, or template-heavy creature.

## 2. Subsystem Development Workflow

Each subsystem should pass through these stages:

### Stage A — Intent

Write:

```text
Subsystem name:
Player-facing purpose:
Fictional purpose:
Primary decision:
Primary mathematical vector:
Secondary interactions:
Out of scope:
```

### Stage B — Smallest Procedure

Write only the minimum rules required to answer:

- What starts the procedure?
- What does the player choose?
- What does the GM establish?
- What is rolled or compared?
- What happens on success?
- What happens on failure?
- What resources or actions are spent?

### Stage C — Interaction Matrix

List interactions with existing systems:

| Existing system | Interaction | Direction of influence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attributes | Which Attribute determines Die Size? | Subsystem reads Attribute | Open/Locked |
| Skills | Which Skill is primary? | Subsystem reads Competency | Open/Locked |
| Equipment | Does equipment add dice, Traits, or Permissions? | Equipment modifies subsystem | Open/Locked |
| Actions | What does it cost? | Subsystem consumes Actions | Open/Locked |
| Resources | What does it consume? | Subsystem consumes resource | Open/Locked |
| Conditions | What interrupts or modifies it? | Condition modifies procedure | Open/Locked |

### Stage D — Mathematical Test

Produce a small script or table testing the likely parameter space.

The output should include:

- Assumptions.
- Formula or method.
- Relevant distributions.
- Edge cases.
- Interpretation limits.
- Conclusions that remain provisional.

### Stage E — Playable Micro-Prototype

Create the smallest playable content sample:

- One or two Skills.
- One or two Classes or Feats, if necessary.
- A few representative enemies or challenges.
- Enough equipment to expose interactions.

Do not write a complete catalogue at this stage.

### Stage F — Stress Test

Ask players or an internal review process to deliberately break the subsystem:

- Maximize one vector.
- Minimize another vector.
- Stack all available effects.
- Use the subsystem outside its intended context.
- Multiclass or combine sources.
- Search for automatic success loops.
- Search for dead choices and dominant choices.

### Stage G — Consolidation

After testing:

1. Separate mechanical problems from presentation problems.
2. Separate framework problems from content problems.
3. Record unresolved questions in the Outstanding Definitions Index.
4. Obtain explicit approval for architectural changes.
5. Update the canonical owner.
6. Update the decision log and project artifacts.
7. Archive superseded versions.

## 3. Documentation Structure Guidance

### 3.1 Canonical rule document structure

A canonical rules file should generally use this order:

```text
Title
Status and scope
Prerequisites or source links
Core definitions
Core procedure
Options and edge cases
Interactions
Examples
Out-of-scope or deferred material
Source links
```

The document should be declarative. It should describe what the current system does, not the abandoned options that led to it.

### 3.2 Research document structure

A research artifact should generally use:

```text
Title
Status
Research question
Sources and assumptions
Method
Results
Limitations
Interpretation
Possible design implications
Links to affected canonical documents
```

Research should distinguish:

```text
Observed result
Interpretation
Design suggestion
Approved decision
```

Only the last category belongs in canonical rules.

### 3.3 Decision-log structure

A decision-log entry should contain:

```text
Decision ID
Date
Topic
Approved resolution
Rationale
Superseded decisions, if any
Affected canonical files
Remaining implementation work
```

The log records why the project chose a direction. It does not become a second rules chapter.

### 3.4 Outstanding-definition structure

Each open item should identify:

```text
Question
Status
Relevant canonical owner
Relevant research
Decisions already made
Remaining choices
Next dependency
```

This prevents a broad label such as “Magic unresolved” from hiding several independent questions.

### 3.5 Summary-document structure

A summary should provide navigation, not duplicate canon.

It should answer:

```text
What are the major subsystems?
Where is each subsystem defined?
What is currently in scope?
What remains open?
```

Detailed formulas, procedures, tables, and edge cases belong in their canonical owners.

## 4. Case-Study Lessons Converted into Project Rules

### From MCDM

MCDM’s public development process is useful for studying tactical system and content iteration; MCDM describes an ongoing series documenting development of its own RPG.[1](https://www.mcdmproductions.com/press)

Apply the lessons by:

- Testing whether combatants create meaningful battlefield changes.
- Giving each Class or enemy role a distinct tactical job.
- Testing boss content separately from ordinary enemy content.
- Asking whether the GM can run the content without excessive lookup or arithmetic.
- Stress-testing optimized combinations instead of only typical builds.

### From Kelsey Dionne

Dionne’s Shadowdark retrospective describes identifying design pillars, discovering that a skill system was not working at the table, and removing it rather than preserving it for compatibility reasons.[1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OADZLovtpPg)

Apply the lessons by:

- Checking every new subsystem against the project pillars.
- Treating removal as a valid outcome.
- Avoiding mechanics added merely to resemble another game.
- Testing whether complexity creates decisions or only bookkeeping.
- Recording explicit out-of-scope boundaries.

### From Ben Milton / Questing Beast

Milton’s discussion of “Rule One” emphasizes the distinctive RPG capability of letting players affect the fictional world by describing actions, with rules and GM adjudication supporting that freedom.[2](https://questingbeast.substack.com/p/rule-one)

Apply the lessons by:

- Designing broad Activities instead of a Skill for every procedure.
- Supporting player-described approaches.
- Treating Requirements and Permissions as tools for preserving open-ended play.
- Avoiding rules that classify actions without adding meaningful decisions.
- Supporting the GM with clear procedures where ambiguity would otherwise create repeated friction.

### From public playtest discussions

D&D Next playtest commentary and broader design discussions emphasize testing both mathematical function and player expectations, and separating the intended experience from what players actually do.[1](https://www.gamesprecipice.com/role-of-playtesting/)

Apply the lessons by:

- Testing stripped-down mathematical prototypes.
- Testing presentation and usability separately.
- Recording what players actually chose, not only what they said they preferred.
- Allowing playtesters to optimize and break the system.
- Comparing feedback against the project’s stated design pillars.

Forum anecdotes should be collected as failure-mode evidence, not adopted as rules.

For each useful anecdote, record:

```text

Context:

```

### Common anecdotal failure categories

Tag forum reports using categories such as:

- Rules ambiguity.
- Character-creation overload.
- Dominant option.
- Dead option.
- Modifier forgetting.
- GM workload.
- Poor pacing.
- Resource imbalance.
- Multiclass exploit.
- Equipment escalation.
- Player expectation mismatch.
- Mathematical instability.
- Poor documentation.

This allows multiple anecdotes to be compared instead of treating one memorable story as proof.

## 6. Project-Specific Operating Model

The current project should use this layered evidence model:

```text
Design pillars:
  What experience are we trying to create?

Canonical framework:
  What procedure are we currently using?

Research:
  What does the procedure mathematically produce?

Prototype:
  Can a small amount of content use it?

Stress test:
  What happens when players optimize or exploit it?

Playtest:
  What happens at an actual table?

Decision:
  What do we approve, revise, defer, or remove?
```

This model supports the current Phase 1 goal: stabilize the fundamental scaffolding before developing large bodies of Classes, Feats, Spells, Ancestries, Equipment, or Monsters.
