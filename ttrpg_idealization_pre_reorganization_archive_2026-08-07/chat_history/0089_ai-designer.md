# Turn 89 — AI-Designer

Spell traditions are a major architectural choice because they can define:

What a caster studies.
What a caster can cast.
How a spell is flavored.
Which classes and paths gain access.
Which Knowledge skills apply.
What kinds of magical problems a character can solve.
How magic differs between cultures and power sources.

The first important distinction is between spell classification and spellcasting tradition.

A spell school describes what a spell does.
A spell tradition describes how a character accesses and performs magic.

A fireball might belong to the evocation school, but be cast through Arcane, Primal, Divine, Shadow, or even Time magic depending on the setting.

1. Useful References
D&D Schools of Magic

The traditional eight schools are:

Abjuration
Conjuration
Divination
Enchantment
Evocation
Illusion
Necromancy
Transmutation
What they do well

D&D schools provide a useful functional taxonomy:

What kind of effect is this?
What spells interact with one another?
What does an antimagic specialist understand?
Which magical effects does a specialist excel at?

They are excellent for labeling spells and designing specialist feats.

What they do poorly

They are not always good spellcasting traditions.

A Wizard can generally access every school.
The schools do not strongly distinguish magical cultures or sources.
Several spells could reasonably belong to multiple schools.
They do not explain why a Divine caster and an Arcane caster perform magic differently.
Best use for our system

Use D&D schools as spell tags or effect categories, not as the primary traditions.

For example:

text
Fireball
Tradition: Arcana, Primal
School: Evocation
Traits: Fire, Area, Projectile

What PF2e risks

Divine can combine healing, curses, protection, and judgment.
Primal can combine combat, healing, movement, and transformation.
Occult can cover mental, spiritual, illusion, and strange magic.

That is mechanically convenient, but it can reduce niche protection.

What it does well

SotDL traditions are excellent for:

Modular path design.

Creating very specific magical identities.

A character is not simply “a wizard.” They might be:

A Death and Curse specialist.
A Battle and War caster.
A Nature and Transformation witch.
An Arcana and Teleportation scholar.

This is highly compatible with your Class/Paths architecture.

What it risks

If traditions are too numerous:

Each individual tradition may have too few spells.
Players may feel forced to collect many traditions to cover basic functions.
Spell access becomes difficult to balance.
The system becomes dependent on large spell catalogs.
“Tradition” can become indistinguishable from “spell school.”
Best lesson

SotDL is probably best used as inspiration for modular magical specialties, not copied as the entire top-level structure.

4. Ars Magica

Ars Magica uses five Techniques and ten Forms.

Techniques
Creo — Create
Intellego — Perceive or Understand
Muto — Change
Perdo — Destroy
Rego — Control
Forms
Animal
Aquam — Water
Auram — Air and Weather
Corpus — Human Body
Herbam — Plants
Ignem — Fire and Heat
Imaginem — Images and Illusions
Mentem — Mind and Spirit
Terram — Earth and Materials
Vim — Magic and Magical Power

A spell is formed by combining a Technique and a Form.

Examples:

Create + Fire
Control + Human Body
Destroy + Mind
Perceive + Magic
Change + Animal
What it does well

Ars Magica separates:

What magic does.
What magic affects.

This is extremely flexible and powerful. It can generate many effects from a compact rules structure.

What it risks
High adjudication demands.
Difficult spell balance.
Players need to understand two intersecting taxonomies.
The system may be too open-ended for a Vancian spell-list structure.
Best lesson

Use a two-axis model if we want deep spell construction:

text
Effect Mode: Create, Perceive, Change, Destroy, Control
Subject: Fire, Body, Mind, Spirit, Nature, Matter, Space, Time

However, this may be better as a spell design framework or crafting subsystem than the primary player-facing tradition structure.

11. Important Design Considerations
A. How many traditions should exist?

Too few traditions:

Characters feel mechanically similar.
All-purpose casters dominate.
Classes lose magical identity.

Too many traditions:

Spell lists become thin.
Characters need many traditions to solve ordinary problems.
Access becomes difficult to understand.
Balancing each tradition becomes labor-intensive.

A reasonable initial target might be:

5 to 8 primary traditions
10 to 20 magical subdomains or specialties
8 or so spell schools/tags
Open-ended Lore specialties for local and cultural magic

The existing provisional five are a strong starting point:

Arcana
Divine
Primal
Shadow
Time

But we should test whether “Time” is truly a tradition or better treated as a rare subject/domain available through several traditions.

B. Are traditions sources, methods, or themes?

These should not be mixed carelessly.

Source-based traditions
Divine
Primal
Spirit
Infernal
Shadow
Method-based traditions
Arcana
Ritual
Alchemy
Rune
Psionics
Subject-based traditions
Time
Fire
Death
Illusion
Blood
Dreams

A clean system might use source/method as the actual Magic Mastery skill, while subject categories are spell tags.

For example:

text
Magic Mastery: Arcana
Spell Domain: Time
School: Transmutation
Delivery: Zone

That is much more flexible than making Time a complete independent tradition.

C. Can traditions overlap?

They should overlap deliberately.

For example:

Arcana and Primal can both cast fire.
Divine and Primal can both heal.
Shadow and Divine can both manipulate death.
Arcana and Time can both teleport or alter sequence.

The difference should be in:

Spell list.
Casting procedure.
Resource use.
Risks.
Available feats.
Social consequences.
What the tradition does especially well.
D. What separates traditions mechanically?

Traditions may differ by:

Attribute pairing.
Spell preparation method.
Required implements.
Resource track.
Spell failure consequences.
Focus interactions.
Range or targeting preferences.
Ritual requirements.
Available reactions.
Spell tags they can access.
How they recover or learn spells.

However, we should be careful not to give every tradition a completely different subsystem. That can create excessive rules burden.

A good baseline might be:

Shared spellcasting procedure.
Shared Vancian slots.
Shared Focus interactions.
Tradition-specific spell access, features, and complications.
A small number of unique tradition rules.
E. What is the relationship between class and tradition?

We have several possible models.

Model 1: Class determines tradition

A Wizard is Arcane. A Priest is Divine.

Advantages:

Strong archetype clarity.
Easy onboarding.

Weaknesses:

Less modular.
Makes multiclassing more restrictive.
Model 2: Tradition is a skill investment

Any character can learn Arcana, Divine, Primal, or another tradition if they gain access.

Advantages:

Strong character freedom.
Supports hybrid casters.
Fits the Universal Actor Schema.

Weaknesses:

Needs careful spell access restrictions.
A fighter may learn magic too easily unless training has meaningful requirements.
Model 3: Class grants access, skill determines proficiency

A class, path, ancestry, feat, mentor, or institution grants access to a tradition. The character then invests in the tradition skill.

This is probably the best fit.

Example:

A Wizard path grants access to Arcana.
A Priest path grants access to Divine.
A Druid path grants access to Primal.
A Shadow initiation feat grants access to Shadow.
A character may multiclass or take feats to gain another tradition.
The actual tradition competency rank determines casting capability.
F. What does tradition access mean?

Access should have several layers:

Access: You are permitted to learn or prepare spells from the tradition.
Training: You have a Magic Mastery skill rank.
Spellbook or Grimoire: You know specific spells.
Slots: You have daily capacity to cast them.
Focus: You can modify, intensify, or improvise them.
Tradition Features: You possess special abilities tied to that tradition.

This prevents “I have Arcana” from automatically meaning “I know every Arcane spell.”

G. What should Vancian preparation do?

Since we are retaining daily Vancian slots, every tradition should answer:

How are spells learned?
How are spells prepared?
Can a character prepare from a spellbook, prayer list, spirit pact, or natural repertoire?
Can spells be exchanged daily?
Do traditions use different preparation times?
Can a caster prepare a spell from another tradition?
What happens if the spell is lost, damaged, forgotten, or forbidden?

A Wizard may prepare from a spellbook. A Priest may prepare through ritual and devotion. A Primal caster may meditate or commune with a living place. A Shadow caster may bind or bargain with entities.

H. What should Focus do?

Focus should not replace spell slots. It can support:

Metamagic
Casting under pressure
Maintaining concentration
Tradition-specific techniques
Emergency Die Step-Ups
Ritual acceleration
Spell shaping
Preventing magical backlash

Traditions may spend Focus differently, but the core Focus economy should remain recognizable.

I. What are the risks of magic?

Since the system rejects meta-currencies, magical risk should use in-world consequences:

Focus loss
Stamina expenditure
Spell slot consumption
Conditions
Corruption
Insanity
Debt to a deity or patron
Spirit attention
Equipment or focus durability
Miscasting
Exhaustion
Social or faction consequences

Not every tradition needs corruption, but each should have an identifiable relationship with risk.

J. How do traditions interact with wounds and combat?

Magic needs to work within the same tactical engine:

Three-action casting.
Reactions and interrupts.
Zones and distance tiers.
Active defenses.
Called shots and anatomical conditions where appropriate.
Damage Absorption.
Conditions rather than endless numerical modifiers.

Spell tags should clarify how a spell interacts with defenses:

text
Fireball
Tradition: Arcana or Primal
School: Evocation
Tags: Fire, Area, Projectile
Target: Reflexes
Effect: Damage and Burning condition

This is the one I would question most strongly.

Time is compelling, but it may work better as:

A rare tradition available to specialized paths; or
A spell domain/tag that Arcana, Divine, Shadow, or other traditions can access; or
A high-tier mastery that only becomes available at Master, Hero, Legend, or Mythic levels.

If Time is available at Level 1, it may create severe balance and conceptual problems around:

Extra actions
Reversing injuries
Rewriting failed rolls
Premonition
Initiative manipulation
Undoing consequences

My provisional recommendation is:

Keep Time as a restricted Magic Mastery tradition or advanced subtradition, not a standard starting tradition.

My current preference is Option C, with five or six broad Magic Mastery traditions and specialist schools or domains layered on top. It gives us:

Strong caster identity.
Modular multiclassing.
Broad enough spell lists.
Space for SotDL-style specialization.
A natural place for D&D schools.
A controlled path for Time and other unusual magic.
Compatibility with Vancian preparation and Focus-based metamagic.
