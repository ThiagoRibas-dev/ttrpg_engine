Matt Colville / MCDM
Best resources
Designing the Game

MCDM has an ongoing development series documenting the creation of its own RPG, including design decisions and development trade-offs.1

This is probably the most directly relevant resource for us because MCDM’s design concerns overlap with ours:

Tactical combat.
Strong class identity.
High-impact abilities.
Boss and monster design.
Dynamic movement.
Negotiation, crafting, and research.
Building a complete system rather than only a player-facing ruleset.
Running the Game

Colville’s older Running the Game material is primarily GM-facing, but it contains many useful design observations about:

Encounter structure.
Player decision-making.
Monster behavior.
Information presentation.
Pacing.
How rules support the intended table experience.

It should be treated as play experience research, not as a system-design authority.

What we should extract

For our project, the most relevant MCDM lessons are:

Build monsters around interesting actions rather than only numerical statistics.
Make combatants change the battlefield.
Ensure classes have a distinctive tactical role.
Test whether a mechanic creates decisions or merely increases output.
Design bosses as a different experience from ordinary enemies.
Observe whether the GM can actually run the content at the table.

MCDM’s public development documentation is particularly useful because it shows design as an iterative process rather than presenting mechanics as if they appeared fully formed.

Kelsey Dionne / Shadowdark

Kelsey Dionne’s Shadowdark design discussions are extremely relevant to our current framework phase.

The most useful principle from her design retrospectives is:

Begin with design pillars, then test whether each mechanic serves those pillars.

In a retrospective discussion about Shadowdark, she describes:

Starting from actual table experience.
Playing a variety of games.
Identifying what she liked and disliked.
Distilling the intended game into design pillars.
Discovering that a skill system was not working well at the table.
Removing it even though she initially felt the game “needed” one to remain adjacent to D&D.
Removing unnecessary inventory complexity.
Using the pillars to identify when a mechanic was working against the intended experience.1

The Arcane Library also maintains an official Shadowdark blog written by Kelsey Dionne, with design ideas, updates, and advice.2

Why this matters for us

This is highly relevant to our current process because we have already made several similar decisions:

Moving from procedure-as-skill to activities.
Removing Mantles as a universal magic axis.
Removing explicit Floors.
Removing [SET] tokens.
Moving old architecture into the archive.
Reconsidering how much equipment should affect the probability curve.

The important lesson is:

A mechanic can be clever, compatible, and theoretically elegant while still being wrong for the game’s intended experience.

Questing Beast / Ben Milton

Ben Milton is useful from a different angle.

His OSR design discussions emphasize:

Player judgment.
Tactical infinity.
In-world problem solving.
Procedures that support actual play.
Avoiding unnecessary rules.
Designing the game around meaningful choices rather than exhaustive simulation.

In a discussion of his “Rule One,” Milton argues that RPGs differ from more closed games because players can affect the fictional world by describing what their characters do, with the GM adjudicating the result.2

That is relevant to our Skill/Activity distinction:

text
Skill:
  Durable field of competence.

Activity:
  What the player is trying to accomplish.

Procedure:
  How the system resolves it.

Judgment:
  How the player chooses an approach in the fictional world.

We do not need to copy the OSR’s preference for minimal rules. But Questing Beast is useful for asking:

Are we writing a rule because the table needs it?
Or because the designer wants to classify everything?
Does the rule create meaningful choices?
Would a broader Activity procedure serve better than another narrow Skill?
Is the GM being supported or replaced by a long procedure?

His newsletter also gathers a wide range of OSR and post-OSR design material, making it useful as a discovery resource rather than a single design doctrine.1

Forum Anecdotes

Forum anecdotes can be valuable, but they need to be treated as qualitative evidence, not authority.

Useful communities include:

RPG Design.
RPG Development.
Tabletop Game Design.
Board Game Design.
System-specific forums.
Designer Discords and postmortem threads.
What forum anecdotes are good for

They can reveal recurring failure modes:

Character creation becoming too long.
Rules that look simple but create table confusion.
Modifiers being forgotten.
Playtesters optimizing in unexpected ways.
Players ignoring intended subsystems.
A mechanic being balanced mathematically but feeling bad.
A subsystem requiring too much GM interpretation.
A rule only working for the designer who already knows its assumptions.
Playtesters confusing design intent with actual procedure.

A useful example is a discussion of RPG design workflows where designers describe a process of:

Establishing design goals.
Brainstorming.
Writing a playable draft.
Playtesting with actual people.
Evaluating whether play meets the original goals.
Returning to the design goals and revising.3

Another useful playtesting discussion emphasizes that the test document should contain the actual mechanics being tested, and that testers should be encouraged to break or optimize the system rather than being guided toward “correct” play.2

What forum anecdotes are bad for

They should not be treated as proof that:

A mechanic is universally bad.
A system is objectively balanced.
A class is objectively overpowered.
A design principle applies to every genre.
A popular game’s solution is automatically correct for ours.

Forum posts are usually:

Anecdotal.
Context-dependent.
Selection-biased.
Often missing the full rules context.
Written after a memorable success or failure.

How These Resources Fit Our Process

They reinforce the process we have already begun:

text
1. Establish design pillars.
2. Define the fundamental model.
3. Separate framework from content.
4. Calculate likely mathematical behavior.
5. Build a small playable prototype.
6. Stress-test it.
7. Observe actual player behavior.
8. Separate math problems from usability problems.
9. Revise.
10. Record only approved decisions as canonical.

For our particular system, the most valuable external lessons are:

From MCDM: tactical roles, active monsters, encounter usability, public iteration.
From Kelsey Dionne: design pillars, removing mechanics that conflict with the intended experience.
From Questing Beast: player judgment, meaningful choices, avoiding rules that classify more than they contribute.
From forum anecdotes: recurring failure modes and real-world playtest friction.
From Vincent Baker: designing from the underlying model rather than isolated mechanics.
From Kobold Guide: broad coverage of RPG design, balance, advancement, magic, pacing, and playtesting.

The most useful addition to our research process would be a dedicated Design Lessons and Postmortems section in the research catalogue, separate from the canonical rules and separate from the decision log.
